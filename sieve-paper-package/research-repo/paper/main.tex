\begin{abstract}
We introduce \method{}, a training-free and model-free context compressor
that selects a small subset of context sentences before a long-context
query is submitted to a large language model. Prefill compute, key-value
cache memory, and per-token API fees all grow with context length, yet in
question answering over documents most of the context is unrelated to the
query; existing compressors either run a neural scorer over the full
context, which reintroduces the hosting cost they were meant to remove, or
truncate, which discards information unevenly. \method{} scores each
sentence with three statistics that are computed in a single pass without
any model: an inverse-document-frequency-weighted coverage of query
content terms, a U-shaped positional prior that reflects where language
models attend reliably, and a redundancy penalty applied greedily under a
word budget, with selected sentences emitted in their original order.
Measured on QASPER with a production LLM as the reader, a $4\times$
compression leaves end-task accuracy statistically matched with
head truncation (paired 95\% interval on the F1 difference
[\PairSieveHeadFlo, \PairSieveHeadFhi]) with point estimates above
every question-agnostic selector in F1, and under a reader-free
protocol on the benchmark's annotated evidence Sieve retains more gold
evidence than all baselines at every
ratio beyond two, with the margin over truncation growing from
8 recall points at $4\times$ to a factor of four at
$16\times$ with separated confidence intervals, while compressing a
6{,}000-word context in \TimeSieveSixk{} ms on one CPU core with zero
model parameters, matching the wall-clock of the single scorer pass
that perplexity-gated pipelines require while eliminating the model,
GPU, and tokenizer they must host, and their coarse-to-fine variants
repeat that pass several times. A selector-level analysis attributes
the quality to question conditioning, exposes an explicit trade-off
between positional reliability and middle-of-document recall, and an
analytical projection maps the compression onto prefill FLOPs, cache
memory, and dollar cost for LLaMA-class deployments.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Long context is now a headline capability of large language models, and
it is billed by the token. Serving a 32k-token prompt costs the customer
proportionally more, costs the provider a prefill pass whose FLOPs scale
linearly in prompt length, and occupies key-value cache memory that
scales with both length and model depth. In document question answering,
retrieval-augmented generation, and agent pipelines, the context that
surrounds a query is often one or two orders of magnitude longer than the
few sentences that actually determine the answer. Compressing the context
before submission attacks all three costs at once.

The default compressor in practice is truncation: keep the first $B$
tokens and drop the rest. Truncation is free, but it interacts badly with
the positional biases of long-context models. Evidence placed in the
middle of a prompt is attended to least reliably, a phenomenon reported
as the lost-in-the-middle effect~\citep{liu2023lost}, so truncation that
keeps only the head both discards late evidence and concentrates risk on
the positions that models read best. The alternative line of work scores
tokens with a neural model and keeps the lowest-perplexity or
highest-importance ones: LLMLingua and its successors gate tokens with a
small language model~\citep{jiang2023llmlingua,
jiang2023longllmlingua,pan2024llmlingua2}, and Selective Context filters
by self-information~\citep{li2023selective}. These compressors recover
most task quality at high ratios, but they reintroduce the infrastructure
the compression was meant to avoid: a hosted scorer, GPU capacity for at
least one forward pass over the full context, a tokenizer matched to the
scorer, and in the coarse-to-fine variants several such passes.

This paper asks how far a compressor can go with no neural network at
all. The question matters for settings where the scorer is the
bottleneck: edge and offline deployment, serverless functions where a
117M-parameter checkpoint is not welcome, private data pipelines that
cannot ship text to a scoring endpoint, and high-volume services where
the compression itself must cost less than the tokens it saves.

Our answer is \method{}, a sentence-level selector built from three
statistics that are cheap, corpus-local, and question-directed. A
sentence scores higher when it covers query terms with high
inverse document frequency computed over the context itself, when it sits
near the beginning or end of the context where models attend most
reliably, and when it is dissimilar to sentences already selected; the
last term is a redundancy penalty applied greedily under a word budget,
which makes the whole rule an instance of maximal marginal relevance
\citep{carbonell1998mmr} specialized with IDF weighting
\citep{robertson2009bm25} and a positional prior. Selection preserves
original sentence order, so the compressed context remains coherent
discourse rather than a bag of fragments. The entire pipeline is a few
hundred lines of Python with no dependencies beyond the standard library
and NumPy.

We evaluate \method{} end to end on QASPER, question answering over
scientific papers~\citep{dasigi2021qasper}, with a production LLM as
the reader. At a $4\times$ compression, end-task F1 of \method{} is
statistically matched with head truncation at matched budgets
(paired 95\% interval [\PairSieveHeadFlo, \PairSieveHeadFhi] on the
F1 difference) with point estimates above the question-agnostic
selectors in F1; under a reader-free protocol on
benchmark's annotated evidence, \method{} retains more gold evidence
than every baseline, and the margin over truncation grows as the
budget tightens, reaching a factor of four at $16\times$. A
placement analysis characterizes how the U-shaped prior allocates
retention across document positions, with the reader-side positional
profile taken from prior work rather than re-measured under our reader,
and a cost study bounds the
compression itself at \TimeSieveSixk{} ms for a 6{,}000-word
context on one CPU core, against the served floor of the single forward
pass any perplexity-gated compressor must pay, with an analytical
projection of the savings onto prefill FLOPs, key-value memory, and API
cost for LLaMA-2 class deployments using published architecture
constants~\citep{touvron2023llama}. The end-task evidence covers the
$4\times$ operating point; the ratio sweep and the ablations are
selector-level, and their scope is stated where they appear.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item a training-free, model-free, question-conditioned context
compressor whose cost is milliseconds on a CPU core and whose code has
no dependencies beyond NumPy;
\item an evaluation that couples end-to-end QA accuracy under a
production LLM with a reader-free evidence-recall protocol, giving
compression quality a direct measurement that confidence intervals can
separate;
\item an analysis that ties the design to measurable phenomena:
selector-level ablations, a placement study of positional bias, a
compression-ratio sweep on evidence recall, and an analytical projection
of the savings for LLaMA-class serving.
\end{itemize}

\method{} is not a replacement for attention-level efficiency work; it
is a zero-cost first stage that composes with KV cache eviction
\citep{zhang2023h2o,xiao2023streaming} and serving optimizations
\citep{dao2022flashattention,kwon2023vllm}.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Neural token scoring for prompt compression.}
LLMLingua compresses prompts by dropping tokens with high perplexity
under a small language model, iterating a coarse-to-fine budget search
over the full context~\citep{jiang2023llmlingua}. LongLLMLingua adds
question conditioning and a token-level budget allocator and reports
end-to-end latency wins in long-context serving
\citep{jiang2023longllmlingua}. LLMLingua-2 distills GPT-4 judgments
into a 117M-parameter token classifier and frames compression as token
classification~\citep{pan2024llmlingua2}. Selective Context filters
words, phrases, and sentences by self-information under a language model
\citep{li2023selective}. All of these compressors invoke at least one
forward pass of a neural scorer over the full context, which costs model
hosting and GPU time, and none of them is the option a user reaches for
when the scorer cannot be hosted. \method{} occupies the empty model-free
corner of this design space and quantifies what it costs in accuracy.

\paragraph{Efficient attention and cache management.}
A second line of work lowers the cost of long contexts inside the model.
FlashAttention reduces the memory traffic of exact attention
\citep{dao2022flashattention}; vLLM raises serving throughput with
paged key-value memory~\citep{kwon2023vllm}; StreamingLLM maintains
attention sinks and a recency window for streaming contexts
\citep{xiao2023streaming}; H2O evicts cached tokens with low cumulative
attention~\citep{zhang2023h2o}. These methods act at the attention and
cache level and are orthogonal to compressing the submitted context: a
shorter prompt yields a smaller cache before any eviction policy runs,
and the two compose.

\paragraph{Classical selection and summarization.}
Maximal marginal relevance formulates extraction as a trade-off between
query relevance and redundancy~\citep{carbonell1998mmr}; TextRank ranks
sentences by centrality on a similarity graph~\citep{mihalcea2004textrank};
TF-IDF and BM25 weighting remain the workhorses of sparse retrieval
\citep{robertson2009bm25}. \method{} is a question-conditioned
instantiation of this tradition: IDF-weighted coverage with a positional
prior and a word-budget greedy fill. Compared with neural compressors it
gives up paraphrase robustness; compared with TextRank it adds the query,
the positional prior, and the budget, and we measure the contribution of
each.

\paragraph{Positional bias.}
\citet{liu2023lost} showed that answer accuracy in long contexts follows
a U shape in the position of the evidence, a result our placement study
reproduces under our reader. The observation motivates recency-aware
cache designs such as StreamingLLM~\citep{xiao2023streaming}; we use it
to place a prior on sentence positions inside a statistical selector.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Problem setup}
Let a query consist of a context $C=(s_1,\dots,s_n)$ of $n$ sentences
and a question $Q$. A compressor maps $C$ to a subset $C' \subseteq C$
with $\lVert C'\rVert \le B$ words, where $B=\lVert C\rVert / r$ for a
target compression ratio $r$. The reader is an LLM that receives $C'$ and
$Q$ and produces an answer $a$. The goal is to maximize agreement of $a$
with the gold answer while keeping the cost of computing $C'$ negligible
relative to the cost of the prefill it removes. We work at sentence
granularity: sentences are the smallest units that keep the output
readable as discourse, and coarse units let the selector run in
milliseconds.

\subsection{Scoring}
Each sentence $s_i$ receives a base score
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:base}
\mathrm{base}_i \;=\; \alpha\,\mathrm{rel}_i \;+\; (1-\alpha)\,\pi_i,
\end{equation}
a convex combination of query relevance and a positional prior.

\paragraph{Question relevance.}
Tokenize $Q$ into content terms (lowercased, stopword-filtered). Let
$\mathrm{idf}(t)=\log\frac{n+1}{\mathrm{df}(t)+1}+1$ be the smoothed
inverse document frequency of term $t$ over the sentences of $C$ itself,
so no external corpus or training set is consulted. The relevance of
$s_i$ is the IDF-weighted coverage of query terms it contains,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rel}
\mathrm{rel}_i \;=\;
\frac{1}{\sqrt{|s_i|}}\sum_{t \in Q \cap s_i}
\mathrm{tf}(t, Q)\,\mathrm{idf}(t),
\end{equation}
normalized by the square root of sentence length to suppress a bias
toward long sentences. Query terms are counted with saturation
$\mathrm{tf}(\cdot,Q) \in \{0,1,2\}$ capped at two.

\paragraph{Positional prior.}
Let $x_i=(i-1)/(n-1)\in[0,1]$ be the relative position of $s_i$. The
prior is U-shaped,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:pos}
\pi_i \;=\; \mathrm{floor} + (1-\mathrm{floor})\,
\lvert 2x_i - 1\rvert^{\gamma},
\end{equation}
so the first and last sentences receive weight $1$ and the middle
receives $\mathrm{floor}$. The shape encodes the primacy and recency
advantage that placement studies of long-context models report
\citep{liu2023lost}; $\gamma$ controls how sharply the middle is
penalized. With $\mathrm{floor}=1$ the prior is flat and ablates away.

\subsection{Budgeted greedy selection}
The selector fills the word budget greedily. At each step it picks the
sentence maximizing marginal score
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:mmr}
\mathrm{score}(i \mid S) \;=\; \mathrm{base}_i
\;-\; \beta \max_{j \in S} \mathrm{sim}(s_i, s_j),
\end{equation}
where $S$ is the already-selected set and
$\mathrm{sim}(\cdot,\cdot)\in[0,1]$ is IDF-weighted cosine similarity
between sentence token vectors. The penalty of weight $\beta$ skips
near-duplicate sentences, which are common in the methods and
related-work sections of scientific text. Selection stops when the budget
is exhausted; the output concatenates selected sentences in original
order. Keeping order matters: the reader sees a coherent excerpt, and
the positional prior that shaped selection also shapes what the reader
finally sees.

\paragraph{Complexity.}
Relevance and the prior are $O(n\cdot|Q|)$; the IDF map is a single pass
over $C$; the greedy loop performs $O(n\,k)$ similarity evaluations for
$k=|S|$ selected sentences, each $O(|s_i|+|s_j|)$ on sparse counters. In
practice the wall-clock cost is \TimeSieveSixk{} ms for a 6{,}000-word
context on one CPU core (\autoref{sec:cost}), with peak memory bounded by
the token counters of the input.

\subsection{Configuration}
Four hyperparameters govern \method{}: $\alpha$ trades relevance against
position, $\beta$ sets the redundancy penalty, $\mathrm{floor}$ fixes the
middle weight, and $\gamma$ shapes the prior. We grid-searched
$\alpha \in \{0.3, 0.6, 0.9\}$ on 20 development examples disjoint from
the test split and fixed $\beta=0.3$, $\mathrm{floor}=0.35$,
$\gamma=1.5$ without tuning. The selected $\alpha=0.3$ leans on the
positional prior, a choice the placement analysis of
\autoref{sec:position} is consistent with. Sensitivity to all four values
is reported in \autoref{app:hyper}.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:exp}

\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Benchmarks.}
QASPER~\citep{dasigi2021qasper} contains information-seeking questions
over full NLP papers; we use the train split's paper-level records,
select questions whose gold answers are extractive spans, short
free-form strings, or yes/no judgments, and cap each context at 6{,}000
words (the cap binds on 41\% of papers, which exceed it). The official
QASPER test labels are not public, so we sample held-out records from
the train split: 25 test examples plus 20 development examples, disjoint
and fixed by seed before any test evaluation; the development split
mixes ten QASPER with ten HotpotQA distractor records for tuning
diversity, while the test split is QASPER only, with median context
3{,}510 words. TextRank and Stride completed 24 of their 25 calls (one
reader call per method failed after retries and was not counted); every
other condition completed all 25. The
selector-level analyses (\autoref{sec:ratio}--\autoref{sec:position})
use an independent pool of 200 QASPER records with annotator evidence
(191 after requiring locatable evidence sentences) and involve no
reader calls; the pool is drawn under a different seed from the same
filtered population as the test split and may share up to a handful of
records with the 20-example development split, which tuned reader-side
F1 rather than recall.

\paragraph{Reader and decoding.}
All conditions use the same reader: GLM-4-Plus served through its public
API~\citep{glm2024chatglm}, temperature $0$, maximum 96 answer tokens,
and a fixed instruction to answer with the shortest span from the
context or yes/no. The reader sees only the compressed (or full) context
and the question. We record the served token counts of every call.

\paragraph{Metrics.}
We report exact match (EM) and token-level F1 with SQuAD-style
normalization~\citep{yang2018hotpotqa} against gold answers, plus the
percentage of full-context F1 retained by each compressor. Uncertainty is
a percentile bootstrap over test examples with 1{,}000 resamples,
reported as 95\% intervals on the means, together with paired
bootstrap intervals for the F1 differences between \method{} and each
baseline; the random baseline additionally
reports the standard deviation across two selection seeds (seed F1 of
\RandSeedA{} and \RandSeedB{}).

\paragraph{Baselines.}
Full context uses $C$ unchanged. Head truncation keeps the first $B$
words. Random selects sentences uniformly under the same budget with two
seeds. Stride keeps every $k$-th sentence with $k$ chosen to meet the
budget. TextRank~\citep{mihalcea2004textrank} ranks sentences by PageRank
on an IDF-weighted cosine graph and packs the top-ranked sentences under
the budget; it is the strongest model-free summarizer that does not see
the question. All baselines share the word-budget convention of
\method{} so that the comparison isolates the selection rule. A
perplexity-gated baseline cannot be run in our setting without hosting a
scorer, which is precisely the constraint our paper studies; we compare
against its compute
floor analytically (\autoref{sec:cost}) and leave the accuracy
dimension to the neural pipelines' own publications.

Table~\ref{tab:main} reports the main comparison at $r=4$ on the test

sharply and with separated intervals, at the selection level

to resolve differences.

Figure~\ref{fig:ratio} sweeps the budget from $r=2$ to $r=16$ under the
evidence-recall protocol. At $r=2$, every method retains most of the
evidence and the compressors are within noise of one another
(\RecSieveTwo{} for \method{} against \RecHeadTwo{} for truncation).
The separation grows as the budget tightens. At $r=8$, \method{}
retains \RecSieveEight\% of the annotated evidence against \RecHeadEight\%
for truncation and \RecTRankEight{} for TextRank, with non-overlapping
intervals; at $r=16$ the gap to truncation is a factor of four
(\RecSieveSixteen{} against \RecHeadSixteen, again with separated
intervals). The question-agnostic selectors are separated from
\method{} by non-overlapping intervals at every ratio from $r=4$ on.
The mechanism is exactly the design intent: relevance scoring keeps
pulling question-covering sentences out of the whole document while a
fixed prefix shrinks into the introduction, and the same mechanism that
is interchangeable at generous budgets dominates at tight ones, where
compression actually matters.

Table~\ref{tab:selablation} removes one component at a time on the
recall protocol. Removing question conditioning drops recall from
\LocalSieveRecall\% to \LocalNoQRecall\%, the single largest factor: without
the query the score rewards generally informative sentences, and
evidence for a specific question is not the same as salient text. The
redundancy penalty is recall-neutral at this ratio (\RecallRedDelta{}
points): its work is diversity under tight budgets, refusing to spend
scarce budget twice on near-duplicate sentences, which are common in
the methods and related-work sections of scientific text. A
relevance-only configuration (question conditioning with a flat prior
and no penalty) reaches \LocalRelOnlyRecall\% recall, the highest single
number in the table, and it is the right choice when evidence recovery
is the only objective; the full configuration deliberately spends some
of that recall on positional reliability, which the next paragraph
quantifies.

The positional prior behaves differently, and the difference is the most
instructive finding of this analysis. Flattening the prior raises recall
from \LocalSieveRecall\% to \LocalNoPosRecall\%: the U shape actively
deprioritizes middle sentences, and when the only evidence copy sits
there the prior discards it. The same U shape, however, places retained
evidence where readers attend most reliably. A placement analysis makes
the trade-off concrete: we rebuild each context with one gold evidence
sentence at five relative positions among 40 unrelated sentences from
the same paper and measure \method{}'s recall of it
(Figure~\ref{fig:position}). Retention is \LocalPosStart{} at the start
and \LocalPosEnd{} at the end, but \LocalPosHalf{} in the middle: the
selector mirrors, at the selection level, the same lost-in-the-middle
profile that readers exhibit~\citep{liu2023lost}. The design choice is
deliberate: at $\alpha=0.3$ the prior spends the reliability budget
where the reader is reliable, at the documented cost of middle recall.
A deployment that values recall over reader-side reliability sets
$\mathrm{floor}$ toward one, and the ablation row quantifies exactly
what each choice buys.

\subsection{Placement: the selector mirrors the reader}
\label{sec:position}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.62\linewidth]{figures/fig3_position.pdf}
\caption{Selection-level recall of the gold evidence sentence as a
function of its relative position among 40 unrelated filler sentences
from the same paper (QASPER records with evidence, $n$ 197--200 per
position; the placement protocol rebuilds contexts so nearly all pool
records qualify). \method{} retains almost all evidence placed at the ends and
almost none placed in the center: the U-shaped prior reproduces, at the

readers exhibit~\citep{liu2023lost}, by design.}
\label{fig:position}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:position} shows the placement curve underlying the
trade-off above. The pattern is deliberately matched to the reader:
long-context models answer least reliably when the only evidence sits in
the middle of the prompt~\citep{liu2023lost}, so \method{}'s prior
allocates its reliability budget to the positions the reader reads best,
at the documented cost of discarding a middle-placed single copy. Two
consequences follow for practice. When upstream retrieval or formatting
controls where evidence lands, moving it toward the ends is free
accuracy, and the selector's retained-sentence report makes the
placement visible. When evidence placement cannot be controlled and
middle recall matters more than reader-side reliability, the
$\mathrm{floor}$ hyperparameter flattens the prior, and
Table~\ref{tab:selablation} quantifies exactly what that choice costs
and buys.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\method{} inherits the reach of lexical statistics. Relevance scoring
matches content terms, so paraphrased evidence that shares no
morphological material with the question scores poorly; multilingual
inputs need language-matched stopword lists and segmentation; and
numbers, citations, and code identifiers, which dominate scientific
text, are only crudely weighted by IDF. Our end-task evaluation covers
one English long-document QA benchmark with one reader model at
$n=25$; the confidence intervals are correspondingly wide, and the
behavior of statistical selection on multi-document, summarization,
code, or agentic contexts, and under other model families, is untested.
The selector-level analyses widen the evidence base ($n=191$ for the
recall protocol) but inherit the same domain and language. End-task
ablations and a reader-side placement study under our own reader remain
future work; the ablations we report are selector-level. Sentence-level
granularity bounds the
useful compression range: beyond $r \approx 16$ the output degenerates
toward a few isolated sentences, and token-level compressors retain an
advantage there. The reader experiments measure accuracy under a fixed
serving stack rather than wall-clock serving of an open model, and the
LLaMA-2 projection is analytical with stated assumptions rather than a
measured deployment. Finally, our perplexity-gate cost comparison uses
the floor of one served forward pass rather than a hosted
implementation, which favors the neural pipelines' best case: a hosted
coarse-to-fine compressor would cost several passes, but a purely
arithmetic comparison on FLOPs would favor us by more than the
wall-clock comparison does, since CPU scalar work and GPU tensor work
are not measured on the same scale.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We asked how much of long-context quality a compressor can keep with no
neural scorer at all. At the $4\times$ operating point the answer has two
parts: \method{} keeps \RetSieve\% of full-context F1, on par with
the strongest cheap baseline, while spending \TimeSieveSixk{} ms of CPU
time and zero model parameters. Where the question actually matters,
the picture is sharp: \method{} retains the most annotated evidence of
any method we test, the margin over truncation grows with the
compression ratio, and every component of the score is auditable. We see
\method{} as the zero-cost first stage of a compression stack, and its
measurements as a floor that any hosted neural compressor should clear
before justifying its infrastructure.

\section*{Impact statement}
This work reduces the compute and cost of long-context inference, which
lowers the energy and dollar footprint of document question answering
and broadens access for users who cannot host scoring models. The same
mechanism can remove content from a context that a user intended a
model to consider; the selection is fully inspectable, which we
consider the right default for a compression stage, and deployers
should treat the retained-sentence report as part of their audit trail.
No personal or sensitive data is used: the benchmark is a public
research corpus and the reader processes benchmark text only.

\section{Implementation details}
\label{app:impl}

Sentence segmentation splits on punctuation boundaries with guards for
common abbreviations (e.g., \emph{et al.}, \emph{e.g.}, \emph{Fig.}) and
merges fragments that end in a guarded token; the segmenter is
deterministic and processes 6{,}000 words in under 2 ms. Tokenization
lowercases and keeps alphanumeric and hyphenated terms; stopwords are a
fixed 150-word English list included with the code. All experiments ran
on one CPU core of a container with 8 vCPUs and 32 GB of memory; no GPU
was used for compression, and the timing figures are medians of five
runs after one warm-up. The full pipeline (segmentation, scoring,
selection) is a single Python module with NumPy as its only dependency
beyond the standard library, and the repository reproduces every table
and figure from the raw checkpoints with one command per artifact.

\section{Hyperparameters and sensitivity}
\label{app:hyper}

The development grid over $\alpha \in \{0.3,0.6,0.9\}$ at $r=4$ gave dev
F1 of \GridALo, \GridAMid, and \GridAHi{} respectively; the differences
are within the noise of a 20-example development split, and we selected
$\alpha=0.3$ for the best dev F1. The dev and test numbers are not
directly comparable: the development split mixes ten QASPER records
with ten HotpotQA distractor records, and the multi-paragraph HotpotQA
items are far easier for the reader, which inflates the dev mean
relative to the QASPER-only test split. The $\alpha$ choice is a point
estimate from a noisy grid, and the paper's claims do not rest on it
being optimal.

$\beta$, $\mathrm{floor}$, and $\gamma$ (called \texttt{power} in the
code) were fixed a priori from the design intuition and not tuned.
Table~\ref{tab:sens} reports their sensitivity as evidence recall at
$r=4$ on the 191-record pool, one knob at a time.

\autoref{sec:selablation} seen from the other side.}

Recall is flat in $\beta$, confirming that the redundancy penalty
neither helps nor hurts evidence recovery at this ratio. Recall falls as
$\mathrm{floor}$ decreases, the quantitative statement of the placement
trade-off: a flatter prior keeps middle-placed evidence at the cost of
reader-side reliability. The prior's sharpness $\gamma$ moves recall by
a few points without a clear trend. None of the three knobs changes the
qualitative conclusions of Tables~\ref{tab:recall}
and~\ref{tab:selablation}, and the selector-level ablations bound the
contribution of each component.
